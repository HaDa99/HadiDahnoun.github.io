## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 The topic and why it matters

Cloud computing has a lot to offer, but its roadmap is full of obstacles and uncertainties. How far and in what way a project should proceed must be decided by the business requirements, not by a product vendor, a cloud provider or cloud experts. The company's business goals must be met in concrete, measurable terms [1, p. 35]. This confirms the scope, approach and overall direction of the project. In other words: cloud solutions must be aligned with business goals.

For this reason, the synergy between cloud computing and IT service management (ITSM) has become indispensable for companies that want to optimize their operations and increase overall efficiency. As companies move more of their IT infrastructure to the cloud, ITSM plays a decisive role in keeping cloud operations running smoothly. This paper underlines the need to integrate ITSM into cloud environments, highlights the considerable benefits of doing so, and shows how ITSM can significantly amplify the advantages of cloud technology.

Effective ITSM practices are needed to cope with the complexity of cloud environments and to keep control over IT services [2, p. 7]. They ensure that the benefits of cloud computing, such as scalability, flexibility and cost efficiency, are maximized, while the risks and costs of data-intensive applications are reduced. Aligning ITSM processes with cloud strategies leads to more effective IT services and secures a competitive advantage in the digital age. These insights form the basis of this paper and motivate a closer look at how ITSM and cloud computing are integrated.

### 1.2 Objective and research question

This paper sheds light on the fundamentals of cloud technology, examines the core principles of ITSM, and analyses how ITSM practices are integrated into cloud environments. The aim is to determine how ITSM can be effectively harmonized with cloud technology to increase the efficiency and effectiveness of cloud operations. The guiding research question is:

> What role does ITSM play today in optimizing cloud operations and advancing a company's IT services, illustrated with an example from software testing?

### 1.3 Structure

- **Section 2** gives an overview of cloud fundamentals, including the service models Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Software as a Service (SaaS), and the benefits of cloud adoption.
- **Section 3** covers the definition, goals and core processes of ITSM and its importance for managing IT services in cloud computing. It also examines cloud challenges that ITSM can help with.
- **Section 4** discusses ITSM in cloud environments for software testing, using an example from the automotive industry, and its impact on performance, stability and strategic alignment.
- **Section 5** summarizes the paper and outlines directions for future research on ITSM in cloud computing.

## 2 Cloud fundamentals

### 2.1 Introduction to the cloud

NIST defines cloud computing as "a model for enabling convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources (e.g., networks, servers, storage, applications, and services) that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction" [3].

The term "cloud" describes a framework in which applications, computing power and storage can be accessed on demand from anywhere in the world. The NIST definition also implies that cloud computing is not a new technology but a model that bundles several existing technologies, such as virtualization, usage-based billing and computing itself, to meet today's market requirements.

Providers such as Google, Amazon, IBM and Microsoft offer large, scalable amounts of resources from data centres over the internet. They have had a major impact on companies and their business models in the IT industry, because this new paradigm has compelling characteristics.

Scalability is one of the most discussed properties: it allows a service to be extended flexibly when demand rises. Another key feature is the pay-as-you-go pricing model¹, which lets businesses rent resources for their own needs without paying to build and maintain their own servers [4, p. 11].

Because cloud services are easy to access, the IT landscape is shifting towards developing software meant to be used widely as a service. Outsourcing services to the cloud can reduce business risk and maintenance costs, since running one's own infrastructure also requires trained staff.

Based on NIST, the cloud offers several features that make it widely accepted by businesses:

- **On-demand self-service:** users acquire and manage computing resources such as server time and network storage without human interaction with the provider.
- **Broad network access:** services can be used across devices and platforms, from mobile devices to desktops.
- **Resource pooling:** the provider's resources are pooled to serve several customers in a multi-tenant model², with resources assigned dynamically or automatically to meet demand efficiently.
- **Rapid elasticity:** resources scale up or down quickly with actual use, which is essential for fluctuating workloads: capacity is available at peak times without unnecessary spending in quieter periods.
- **Measured service:** a transparent pay-as-you-go model lets users track and optimize their resource use and align spending directly with their needs [3][4].

Together, these properties make cloud computing a powerful, flexible and cost-effective solution for modern IT requirements that supports digital transformation across industries.

<small>¹ Pay-as-you-go: users order cloud resources from a larger pool and pay per use.
² Multi-tenant model: shared hosting in which customers share computing resources in a public or private cloud [12].</small>

### 2.2 Cloud architecture and service models

To understand cloud computing, one has to distinguish between the service models. According to Figure 1 (software computing architecture [4, p. 9]), a cloud computing system consists of four layers:

- **Hardware layer:** usually implemented in data centres. Typical concerns are hardware setup, fault tolerance, traffic management and the management of power and cooling.
- **Infrastructure layer:** a pool of compute and storage resources, often called the virtualization layer. Virtualization enables dynamic resource allocation, i.e. automatic adjustment of CPU, memory, storage and network bandwidth to optimize bandwidth use, energy consumption and so on. The **IaaS** model is based on this layer: the consumer can provision processing, storage, network and other fundamental resources and install and run any software on them, including operating systems and applications [3].
- **Platform layer:** operating systems and application frameworks on top of the infrastructure. Its purpose is to reduce the complexity of deploying applications directly into VM containers. IBM Cloud Foundry, for example, works at the **PaaS** layer and offers APIs that simplify developing, deploying and scaling cloud-native applications across infrastructures. In PaaS the provider manages the platform infrastructure and gives customers a complete platform of hardware, software and infrastructure.
- **Application layer:** at the top of the stack, the actual cloud applications. Unlike traditional applications, they can use automatic scaling to improve availability and performance and to reduce costs. **SaaS** lets users use applications developed by the provider and deployed on a cloud infrastructure, accessed via a web browser or an API from many client devices. Users do not manage or control the network, servers, operating systems or storage [18, ch. 2].

## 3 IT service management (ITSM)

This section introduces ITSM and the ITIL framework, then turns to the challenges of using cloud technology and their significance for ITSM, and finally explains the core processes relevant to addressing them.

### 3.1 Definition and goals of ITSM

In the context of cloud computing, ITSM refers to the structures, processes and strategies companies use to manage and deliver IT services with cloud technology. The *Handbuch IT-Management* [5] defines ITSM as the process of planning, monitoring and controlling the quality and quantity of delivered IT services, while maintaining the goals of business-process and customer orientation as well as cost optimization. In cloud computing, this means ensuring that IT services match business goals while providing security, cost efficiency and performance, as cloud computing itself keeps evolving.

### 3.2 What challenges does cloud computing pose for ITSM?

Cloud computing offers the IT industry considerable opportunities, but it also brings challenges that need to be solved carefully.

**Compliance and governance.** Cloud computing raises governance and compliance questions, especially around resource management. Well-managed resources such as storage, compute and bandwidth can be distributed effectively to meet service requirements and comply with company standards and laws. This requires a governance structure that aligns resource use with compliance requirements and business goals. ITSM is crucial here because it provides best practices for handling IT resources and services. In change management, traditional approval processes can limit the IT department's ability to expand cloud services quickly and on demand, yet rapid growth can lead to unexpected additional costs. A balance must be found between the cost of cloud-based changes and their approval. Using ITIL-based standard change models for cloud scenarios can make the change process more flexible [6, p. 10].

**Reliability and downtime (failure in the cloud).** The article "Cloud failure prediction based on traditional machine learning and deep learning" [7] covers several aspects of reliability, including the need for robust fault-tolerant architectures, redundancy and failover mechanisms to minimize downtime. Figure 2 summarizes the failure causes it considers.

Unplanned reboots, software bugs and cyberattacks are common causes of software failures that lead to service failure. Services can also fail at scheduling time, usually due to software bugs or resource overload. Power outages, system malfunctions and storage problems that threaten long-term operation are examples of hardware failures. Failures can also be independent and caused by external factors such as human error (misconfiguration, operator mistakes) or environmental factors (overheating, natural disasters). Correlated failures, such as power outages, affect entire infrastructures.

By adopting ITSM practices, companies can handle and resolve incidents systematically, identify and eliminate root causes and manage changes to the IT environment. *Service Level Management in Cloud Computing* [8, pp. 118–129] discusses approaches to reliable monitoring that ensure high availability and reliability of cloud services: methods for estimating availability, dealing with measurement errors and choosing monitoring intervals that balance precision and performance impact. Further ITSM practices apply in incident and problem management and in service continuity management.

There are many more challenges, such as vendor lock-in, scalability and security. Resource management can become very broad, so this paper focuses on the points relevant to the case study in Section 4 (software testing in the cloud). The next section shows which core ITSM processes address these challenges.

### 3.3 Selected core ITSM processes based on ITIL

ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library) is a collection of ITSM practices focused on aligning IT services with business needs. It describes processes, procedures, tasks and checklists that ensure a baseline of competence [16]. Version 3 (2011) consists of five books covering five areas.

A central element of ITIL is the **service lifecycle** (Figure 3, based on [17, p. 36]), used to manage and improve IT services. Its five phases are shown as a circular flow, reflecting the continuous nature of service management [5]:

- **Service Strategy** sets the overall ITSM approach and aligns IT services with business goals (e.g. service portfolio management).
- **Service Design** covers turning strategic requirements into new or changed IT services. It includes **Service Level Management (SLM)**: planning, developing, establishing and monitoring service level agreements between the parties, with the aim of ensuring an agreed quality level for all IT services delivered to users [8, p. 23]. A **Service Level Agreement (SLA)** is an agreement between the cloud service provider (CSP) and the cloud service consumer (CSC) that sets out the expected service level the CSP promises to deliver [18, ch. 11]. SLAs ensure a defined overall quality of service (QoS), which Schmitt [10] defines as well-defined, controllable system behaviour in terms of quantitative parameters. Within SLM, relationship management plays an important role: organizing, using and maintaining relationships with customers, suppliers and other stakeholders, especially when communicating with service providers about SLAs and SLA violations.
- **Service Transition** moves services from design into operation, including commissioning new services. It covers change management, release and deployment management, and service asset and configuration management. One example is applying change management to SaaS subscription management: to benefit from the cloud's scalability and rapid elasticity, cloud changes should be pre-approved within defined limits. This includes deciding whether to accept requests for additional SaaS subscriptions and controlling the related spending efficiently [6, Example 2: Change Management].
- **Service Operation** focuses on the day-to-day running of services so that they are delivered effectively and efficiently. It includes incident management, problem management and access management. Reliability and downtime (Section 3.2) matter here: incident management aims to minimize the negative impact of incidents and restore the defined operating state at the agreed service quality, through to documenting the incident. Problem management is closely linked: it aims to reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents. Incident management provides temporary workarounds, while permanent solutions for known problems are developed and implemented through change management. Figure 4 shows this sequence: one or more repeated incidents point to the cause of a problem (known root cause), which is recorded as a known error and fixed structurally through change management [5, 10.3.1.2 & 10.3.1.3].
- **Continual Service Improvement** continuously improves services based on operational feedback and changing needs.

## 4 How ITSM supports cloud use in software testing

In the automotive industry, test strategy keeps evolving with the growing number of software-controlled vehicle functions. Since there is no other way to test increasingly advanced functions with many variants and fast turnaround, virtual scenarios are used more and more in developing ECU functions, and testing them reliably becomes ever more important. The cloud has a lot to offer for more efficient virtual testing.

Continuous testing in the cloud has spread recently because code is becoming more complex and release cycles shorter. Different cloud models are used depending on the test scope:

- **SaaS**, or the derived model STaaS (Software Testing as a Service) [14], provides tools for managing test cases, test runs and defects.
- **PaaS** provides environments for developing, testing and deploying applications, so developers and testers can manage the application lifecycle without worrying about infrastructure.
- **IaaS** lets the consumer build virtual test environments themselves, using the cloud's flexibility and scalability.

Pure cloud test instances need a data infrastructure holding metadata, test data sets and the software under test, i.e. SaaS combined with cloud data storage.

Cloud technology also speeds up development and testing because it makes offshore collaboration easier: testing and development can run around the clock at sites worldwide on shared cloud resources (e.g. BMW Group's joint ventures in Romania and India).

Cloud software testing makes it possible to automate testing faster and more easily, to use the cloud's scalability without the cost and complexity of physical test infrastructure, and to use real-time feedback and monitoring that speed up finding and fixing defects. This section looks at how ITSM can support this use case.

ITSM already matters before test instances are migrated to the cloud, because the migration is not trivial. It changes established ways of working and can meet resistance from employees [14], so change management cannot be neglected. The study "When to migrate software testing to the cloud?" [15] lists relevant factors, such as test case dependencies: test cases should be independent or have easily identifiable dependencies to allow parallel execution. ITSM change management practices are therefore essential for a structured, planned transition.

### 4.1 The importance of service level management

To make sure cloud services meet the strict testing requirements and quality standards of automotive software development, it is worth looking at how ITSM supports SLM for cloud software testing, from defining the SLA to monitoring it. Handling SLA violations is covered in Section 4.2.

#### 4.1.1 Planning: defining and creating the SLA

The case considered is a SaaS for managing test cases, test runs and defects in software quality assurance. The SLA is usually signed between the SaaS provider, responsible for maintaining the service infrastructure and providing support, and the customer, here a software development company subscribing to the SaaS.

Defining the SLA starts with the requirements relevant to the service. For a software testing service, high system availability, data integrity and response times are especially important. These standards must be clearly defined to ensure the safety requirements and functionality of critical vehicle systems. Real-time data processing, highly available storage and robust network infrastructure should be identified as critical services. These QoS metrics are mostly performance-related; the SLA should also cover remedies, penalties and the scope of testing services, including the expected number of releases or build iterations within a given time, plus further points on responsibilities.

In this phase, the relationship manager will, for example, ask the contractor's business unit which key metrics are already being monitored.

#### 4.1.2 Implementation

After the contract and technical setup, the client's staff must be trained to understand the SLA requirements and help ensure compliance. Change management is needed to make sure everyone who needs training gets it: not only technical staff but also QA teams, project managers and everyone else involved in the affected processes. Change management also matters when the SLA changes.

"Simulation of SLA-based VM-scaling algorithms for cloud-distributed applications" [13] presents SLA-based scaling of cloud services. By linking SLA guarantees to the actual number of VMs available to cloud applications, their QoS metrics and the scope and frequency of tests, the cloud management system (CMS) can scale VMs more effectively when it uses SLAs to describe application performance.

#### 4.1.3 Monitoring: SLA monitoring and compliance

Monitoring measures the availability and performance of an application and its underlying components, and the resulting data is the basis for SLA compliance. Many incidents and challenges can cause SLA violations at the SaaS provider. The article [9] proposes a two-stage SLA management process that first supports the choice of provider and then provides continuous, risk-based offline and online monitoring of SLAs to detect all service violations.

Monitoring can run into challenges such as data integrity and security issues. Ensuring integrity and security usually requires extra system resources, but it is critical for automotive software testing. A 2024 paper [11] proposes several solutions. A key recommendation is improved Proof of Retrievability (PoR) schemes that minimize resource consumption while verifying the reliability and integrity of stored data, so that data in the cloud stays intact and unchanged. Encrypted audit protocols offer a secure, effective way to respond to anomalies and security breaches without hurting system performance.

ITSM tools such as ServiceNow and BMC Helix ITSM include strong monitoring interfaces. They make it easier to automate processes and, above all, to report to stakeholders or the relationship manager. This shows how ITSM practices help focus on risk management, data integrity and security, and resource use through optimized monitoring.

### 4.2 ITSM for handling "cloud failure"

In cloud software testing, where high availability and continuous operation are essential, the ability to react to and resolve problems quickly is critical.

When SLA violations are caused by architectural or resource failures, or a cloud service goes down, ITSM tools combined with automated monitoring help distinguish and classify incidents (Figure 5, based on [8, p. 72]).

In software testing, incidents are not only cloud service interruptions but also defects in the software under test, which also need to be found quickly. ITSM tools play a key role in speeding up software validation. In testing advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS), for example, incidents such as delayed braking responses are recorded and analysed systematically with ITSM tools. These tools run trend analyses to detect patterns and help locate underlying issues, such as latency in sensor fusion algorithms under high load. Their problem management functions route these findings to the right engineering teams for in-depth analysis and resolution. They also make it easier to update test protocols to prevent potential issues, improve test efficiency and reduce interruptions. This systematic approach significantly improves test depth and quality in the automotive industry.

To deal with the most important problems first, prioritization and attention to detail are needed. ITSM tools support predefined escalation procedures and make sure the right people are involved to resolve issues quickly.

Beyond incident management, ITSM tools enable problem management by analysing incident records to detect trends and underlying problems. This helps catch potential issues before they affect testing and reduces the frequency and severity of test interruptions.

## 5 Summary and outlook

### 5.1 Summary

This paper examines how ITSM is essential not only for managing but also for increasing the efficiency of cloud operations, ensuring that IT services match and support companies' strategic goals in digital environments. Based on the ITIL framework, several key ITSM processes relevant to the challenges of cloud applications were presented, and a specific example of software testing in the cloud was discussed.

### 5.2 Further research

Multi-cloud use was not covered. There are open questions about how ITSM can be implemented effectively in multi-cloud environments, and some studies discuss cloud brokerage and multi-cloud as challenges for ITSM.

Another important topic is integrating AI into ITSM tools, especially given the rapid development of AI in recent years. AI algorithms will make it much easier to detect patterns in monitoring and in problem and incident management and to act early. AI has already been used in research to study fault tolerance and isolate the causes of cloud failures. Detecting overload and enabling VM placement and load balancing that use resources better, and are therefore more sustainable, will remain important.

AI is already partly used in everyday IT operations to automate ticket classification, incident matching and handling user requests, around the clock and without human interaction. Could AI go further? It could support user-centred services that simplify development, migration and use, for example through low-code or no-code platforms for running ITSM applications efficiently.
